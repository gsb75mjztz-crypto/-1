# InvestorHub — Monetisation Principles

The test behind every item below is the same one used throughout this project: **would this survive being fully disclosed to the user, in plain language, on the same page where it applies?** If a monetisation mechanic only works because the user doesn't know it's there, it doesn't belong in this product — not for ethical branding reasons, but because the entire business case rests on being the one platform in this category that's structurally not conflicted. That asset is slow to build and fast to lose, and revenue decisions are the most likely place it gets spent by accident.

## 1. Revenue Ladder

Sequenced so each stage is only pursued once the prior one has real usage behind it — monetising too early, on too little trust or data, is a bigger risk than monetising too late.

- **Stage 0 — Free core, no revenue** (launch → early traction). Comparison tool, calculator, fund pages, newsletter. Entirely free, no ads, no affiliate links. The job at this stage is proving the product is genuinely useful and building the trust asset — not extracting value from it yet. Revenue here would be premature and would risk shaping product decisions around monetisation before the product itself is proven.
- **Stage 1 — Newsletter sponsorship** (once there's a real, engaged subscriber base). The lowest-risk revenue on the ladder because it's clearly delineated, separable from the product's factual content, and easy to label unambiguously (see Section 4). Does not touch fund data, comparisons, or rankings.
- **Stage 2 — Premium tools subscription** (once free tools have proven retention). Deeper analytics, more funds covered, portfolio-level tracking, saved history — paid tier sits alongside a free tier that remains genuinely useful on its own (see Section 5). This is the first "real" revenue line and should become the primary one over time.
- **Stage 3 — Transparent, clearly-labelled affiliate links** (only once Stage 2 revenue is established, not before). Introduced late and cautiously, under the strict policy in Section 3 — never earlier, because affiliate revenue is the single mechanic most likely to quietly bend product decisions if it becomes financially necessary too soon.
- **Stage 4 — B2B / licensing** (schools, employers, financial-wellness programmes). Licensing the education content and tools to institutions. Genuinely additive — it doesn't touch the consumer product's neutrality at all, since the institution is the customer, not an advertiser inside the product.

**Deliberately excluded from the ladder entirely:** display advertising and paid placement in comparison/screener results. Both are covered under Section 2 — not "later," but never.

## 2. Things We Will Never Do

Written down now, before any revenue pressure exists, specifically so it can't be quietly renegotiated later under pressure.

- Never accept payment to influence which fund appears first, rank higher, or gets shown at all in a comparison, screener, or search result. Ordering is always by the data (fee, size, performance, alphabetical) — never by who's paying.
- Never let an affiliate relationship change what data is shown or how it's framed. A fund with an affiliate deal attached gets the exact same fee/holdings/performance display as one without.
- Never run display advertising. Ads on a page presented as neutral financial information create exactly the ambiguity this product exists to remove.
- Never accept sponsored content that isn't visibly, unambiguously labelled as sponsored — no "native" blending into editorial content.
- Never use user portfolio or watchlist data commercially (sold to third parties, used to target product placement, etc.) beyond what's needed to run the feature the user asked for.
- Never let a premium paywall gate the free tier down to the point of being misleading-by-omission — e.g. showing a fee comparison but hiding the actual number unless you subscribe. Free tier stays honest and complete on the fundamentals; premium adds depth, not gates basic facts.
- Never publish a "top ETFs" or "best funds" list that's actually a paid or affiliate-influenced placement dressed as editorial judgement.

## 3. Affiliate Policy

Affiliate revenue is the one on this list with the most real-world track record of quietly corrupting otherwise well-intentioned finance content, so it gets the strictest handling.

- **Timing:** not introduced until Stage 3 of the ladder — i.e. not until the business has a primary revenue line that doesn't depend on it, so affiliate decisions are never made under financial pressure.
- **Scope:** limited to broker/platform sign-up links (e.g. "open an account with X"), never to specific fund purchases. Recommending or favouring a fund for affiliate reasons is the one line that most directly threatens neutrality; a broker sign-up link sits one step removed from fund-level advice.
- **Disclosure:** every affiliate link labelled inline, at the point of the link, in plain language — "This is a paid link; we may earn a commission if you sign up" — not buried in a footer disclosure page.
- **No exclusivity:** never structure a deal that requires only promoting one broker, or requires not mentioning a competitor. If a broker's terms would require that, the deal isn't taken.
- **Never inside the comparison or screener tools themselves.** Affiliate links, if they exist at all, live in clearly separate contexts (e.g. a "how to start investing" guide), never attached to or inside the fund-comparison data itself.
- **Ongoing check:** periodically audit whether affiliate presence correlates with any change in tone, coverage, or framing of the affiliated broker versus others — a formal check against drift, not just a one-time policy.

## 4. Sponsored Content Policy

- **Structural separation, not just a label.** Sponsored content lives in a clearly distinct section/format from editorial and data pages — never styled to resemble a fund page, comparison result, or guide.
- **Labelling standard:** "Sponsored" or "Paid partnership" stated at the top of the content, before the user starts reading — not a small tag easy to miss, and not industry-jargon like "in partnership with."
- **Content control:** the sponsor can pay for placement and visibility; they never get to dictate or review claims made about specific funds elsewhere on the site, and sponsored content itself cannot contain a personal recommendation or suitability claim (same advice-boundary rule that governs the rest of the product — see the earlier regulatory discussion).
- **No sponsorship of the comparison tool, calculator, screener, or any fund data page, ever, regardless of price offered.** These are the trust core of the product and are structurally off-limits to commercial influence, full stop — not a negotiable tier.
- **Newsletter sponsorship** (Stage 1 on the ladder) follows the same labelling rule: a clearly marked, separate block, never blended into the "concept of the week" editorial content.

## 5. Premium Philosophy

- **Free tier must stand alone as genuinely useful, indefinitely** — not a crippled trial designed to frustrate people into upgrading. Fee comparisons, basic fund data, the compounding calculator, and core comparisons stay free permanently. This matters for two reasons: the active-beginner audience this product targets often can't or won't pay early, and a stingy free tier undermines the exact trust-building goal the whole go-to-market strategy depends on.
- **Premium adds depth and convenience, not access to withheld facts.** Good candidates: expanded fund coverage beyond the curated 30–50, saved portfolio tracking over time, deeper historical/overlap analysis, export tools, priority data refresh. Bad candidates, never to be gated: the fee number itself, whether a fund is a personal recommendation (never offered regardless of tier), basic comparison functionality.
- **Pricing should be honest about who it's for.** This audience is not flush with disposable income — premium should be priced for "a committed intermediate investor who's outgrown the free tools," not positioned as something an active beginner is nudged to feel they're missing out on. A product that makes a 19-year-old with £300 invested feel inadequate for not paying is working against its own mission.
- **No dark patterns in the upgrade path** — no fake urgency, no artificial scarcity, no "recommended" plan pre-selected to be the most expensive one by default. The upgrade decision should be as calm and clearly-reasoned as everything else the product asks users to do.
- **Cancellation as easy as sign-up.** One click, no retention maze, no "are you sure" guilt sequences. Consistent with treating the user as a capable adult managing their own money, not someone to be manipulated into staying.
